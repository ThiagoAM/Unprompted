The Tide Machine

A tide is a sum of cosines. In 1872 that fact was turned into brass and wire. This page rebuilds the machine in software, runs it on real data, and checks the answer.

What the wire is doing

The ocean does not slosh at one frequency. It responds to a whole catalogue of astronomical rhythms at once: the moon crossing your meridian, the sun doing the same, the moon's distance breathing in and out over 27.55 days, the plane of its orbit wobbling over 18.6 years. Each rhythm arrives at a fixed frequency set by celestial mechanics — but with an amplitude and a lag that belong to your particular harbour, because they depend on the shape of the basin the water has to move through.

So the height of the water at a place is a sum of cosines. Every term has a frequency you can compute from the sky and two numbers you can only get by measuring that harbour for a year or more. Boston's principal lunar term has an amplitude of 1.371 m; Honolulu's is 0.169 m. Same moon, different room.

Once the tide is a sum of cosines, predicting it stops being oceanography and becomes arithmetic — the tedious kind. Thirty-seven cosines, evaluated every hour, for a year, for every port that matters. In the 1870s that was thousands of hours of human calculation. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, noticed that you do not need to calculate a sum of cosines if you can build one.

Brass, 1872

The trick is the wire. Fix one end. Thread it alternately over a pulley that can move and under a pulley that cannot, over, under, over, under, down the length of the machine, and tie the free end to a pen. Now raise one of the movable pulleys by a distance d: the wire has to find 2d of slack from somewhere, and the only place is the pen, which drops. Raise several, and the pen sums them. The wire performs the addition, all terms at once, at the speed of sound in steel.

Each movable pulley rides on a crank turned by a gear train cut so its wheel turns at that constituent's frequency. The pin is set at a radius equal to that constituent's amplitude and clocked to its phase. Turn the handle and the pen draws the tide.

Thomson designed and built the first one in 1872–73 with Edward Roberts and Alexander Légé; the prototype shown to the British Association in 1873 handled eight components, and a ten-component version followed in 1875–76 and now sits in the Science Museum in South Kensington.1 Roberts's 1879 machine took twenty, extended to twenty-four in 1881. The machines kept growing because the catalogue of rhythms kept growing. The Doodson–Légé machine, delivered in December 1950, had forty-two.2

Position Z

On 9 October 1943 a three-page handwritten letter marked MOST URGENT reached Arthur Doodson at the Liverpool Tidal Institute. It came from William Farquharson at the Admiralty, and it contained eleven pairs of harmonic constants — amplitudes and phases — for a place identified only as Position Z. Farquharson wanted hourly heights for four months beginning 1 April 1944.3

Doodson was not told where Position Z was. He was the world's leading authority on tides and he guessed. What he was not told, and could not have guessed, is that the constants he had been sent were partly fictional: there was almost no data for that stretch of coast, and Farquharson had assembled them by bending the constants for Le Havre until they matched fragments of tide curve brought back by reconnaissance. He said so plainly in the letter:

“The place is nameless and the constants inferred. There is in fact very little data for it. I am gambling on the inferred shallow-water constants giving something like the right answer.”3

Doodson set the cranks on two brass machines at Bidston Observatory — a Kelvin-type and a Roberts-designed one built in 1906 — and turned the handles.3 The landing beaches he was computing for have a range that exceeds six metres, which decides how much open sand a soldier has to cross under fire. The gamble held.

Does it actually work

The machine above is not a picture of a machine. The cranks turn at frequencies derived from the moon's and sun's mean longitudes; the pin radii and phases are the published harmonic constants for the station you have selected; and the pen height is their sum. So it can be checked, and it should be.

NOAA publishes both the harmonic constants for its stations and its own official tide predictions. I took the constants, computed the whole of 2026 independently, and compared, hour by hour, against what NOAA says the tide will do — — hourly heights across ten stations, plus every high and low water NOAA lists for the year.

Nine of the ten agree to within two centimetres RMS each, on tides of half a metre to six metres. Taken together they match NOAA to 0.98 cm RMS across 78,840 hourly values, and place 12,317 high and low waters within 0.96 minutes and 0.61 cm on average. Anchorage is the exception, and an instructive one. Cook Inlet is long, shallow and funnel-shaped, and it mangles the tide into something a short list of astronomical frequencies cannot describe: NOAA publishes 120 constituents for Anchorage rather than 37, most of them shallow-water compounds like 3MS8 and 4MSK11 that exist only because the basin is nonlinear. Feed it the 37 the other stations use and you get the tide wrong by 22 cm. That is still 0.25% of a nine-metre range, and it is visibly the right shape — but it is the honest edge of the method.

Those ten stations are the ones the page ships with, so they are also the ones I could have accidentally tuned to. The check that matters is the one made afterwards, on stations that played no part in building it: Key West, San Diego, New London, Pago Pago, Sand Point in the Aleutians, Bermuda, Charlotte Amalie and Ocean City Inlet — for 2031, a year never fetched while the code was being written. Every one of them agrees with NOAA to within 1.6 cm RMS. The script that does this is next to this page; it fetches everything live, so you can point it at any station you like and watch it succeed or fail on its own.

There is one more place the numbers look worse than they are. At Galveston the largest timing disagreement in the year is 90 minutes, which sounds alarming until you look at the curve: over the half hour around that high water the water moves 0.7 mm. Galveston's tide is diurnal, and near the turn it is so flat that "the time of high water" stops being a well-defined quantity. Thirty-eight of its 1,059 events are like that. Both answers are right; the question is soft.

The part that was hard

Almost none of the difficulty is in the sum of cosines. It is in the fact that the sky is not quite periodic. The plane of the moon's orbit precesses with an 18.61-year period, and as it does, it swings the effective amplitude and phase of every lunar constituent. The corrections are called the node factor f and the nodal angle u, and they are what separates a plausible tide curve from a real one.

My first attempt was wrong by 63 cm RMS — worse than useless. The diagnosis is worth recording, because the error had a signature. Fitting NOAA's own curve back onto my basis function showed that every constituent's amplitude was right to a millimetre, and that every constituent with u = 0 — the solar ones, S2, K1, P1, Sa — had its phase right to a fifth of a degree. Everything lunar was wrong, and wrong in exact proportion: M4 by twice M2's error, M6 by three times. That is not a bug in the tide model; it is a single broken angle, multiplied. Solving the little system gave it up: one of the two nodal angles was off by 18.45°, and the other was fine.

The second problem was a constituent called M1, and it is a more interesting kind of wrong. Its node factor matched NOAA's to four decimal places across seven years spanning a two-fold range — so the magnitude formula was certainly right — while its phase was near enough to 180° out. That combination is a fingerprint: the node factor depends on cos 2P, which is blind to the sign of the angle, so a sign error hides in the amplitude and shows up only in the phase. Testing the two conventions against ten stations settled it in one run — every station improved by roughly a factor of eight at once, which is the shape of a right answer rather than a tuned one.

None of that was reasoning I could have done from the inside. It came from having the ground truth to subtract.

Notes

Data. Harmonic constants, datums and reference predictions come from NOAA CO-OPS (tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov), fetched during this session. Heights are metres above mean lower low water; the constants are relative to mean sea level, so each station carries its own MSL−MLLW offset. Times are UTC.

Method. Equilibrium arguments are built from Doodson coefficients on the mean longitudes of the moon, sun and lunar perigee, using Meeus polynomials; node factors and nodal angles follow Schureman. Unlike NOAA — which freezes f and u at mid-year, a holdover from doing this by hand — this page recomputes them continuously. Adopting NOAA's convention instead would lower the residuals slightly further; the continuous version is reported because it is the one the page actually runs.

What isn't here. This predicts the astronomical tide only. Real water also responds to wind, atmospheric pressure and river discharge, which routinely move it by more than every error on this page combined. A storm surge does not care what the moon is doing. L2 and M1 use Schureman's special forms and are the least certain terms; Anchorage is knowingly short 83 constituents. Do not navigate by this.
